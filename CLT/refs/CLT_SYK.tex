\begin{abstract}
Fix an even interaction order $q\geq4$, and let $Z_N(\beta)$ denote the
partition function of the Gaussian Sachdev--Ye--Kitaev model with $N$
Majorana fermions.  We prove that for all sufficiently small inverse temperatures
$\beta > 0$, there exists $\rho=\rho(\beta)>0$ such that   
\[
N^{q/2 -1}(\log Z_N(\beta)-\E \log Z_N(\beta))
  \Longrightarrow \N(0,\rho^2).
\]  
\end{abstract}

\noindent\emph{Keywords:} Sachdev--Ye--Kitaev model,
free energy, central limit theorem, Gaussian chaos.

\section{Introduction and main results}
\label{sec:introduction}



\subsection{The SYK model}\label{subsec:SYK-model}

Fix an even integer $q\geq4$, and let $N$ be even.  Write
\[
  \calI_N=\{I\subset[N]:|I|=q\},\qquad
  \sigma_N^2=\frac{(q-1)!}{N^{q-1}},
\]
and let $G= (g_I)_{I\in\calI_N}$ be independent standard Gaussian random
variables.  Let $\psi_1,\ldots,\psi_N$ be Majorana matrices satisfying
$\{\psi_i,\psi_j\}=2\delta_{ij}\I$.  For
$I=\{i_1<\cdots<i_q\}$ set
\[
  \psi_I=\mathrm i^{q/2}\psi_{i_1}\cdots\psi_{i_q}.
\]
Then $\psi_I$ is Hermitian, $\psi_I^2=\I$, and $\norm{\psi_I}=1$.  Define
\[
  H_N(G)=\sigma_N\sum_{I\in\calI_N}g_I\psi_I,
  \qquad
  Z_N(z;G)=\tr e^{-zH_N(G)},
  \qquad
  F_N(z)=\E Z_N(z;G).
\]
Here $\tr=2^{-N/2}\operatorname{Tr}$ denotes the normalized trace; in
particular, $Z_N(0;G)=F_N(0)=1$.  This normalization does not affect the
relative fluctuations studied below. We sometime write $Z_N(\beta) = Z_N(\beta;G)$ for brevity. 
For real $\beta$, set
\[
  Y_N(\beta;G)=\frac{Z_N(\beta;G)}{F_N(\beta)}.
\]
Thus $\E Y_N(\beta)=1$.
Throughout, all limits are taken as $N\to\infty$ through even integers.

\subsection{Main results}
It is studied the limiting behavior of the logarithmic partition function in high-temperature regime:
\begin{theorem}
There exists $\tilde{\beta}_q>0$ such that for any $\beta\in [-\tilde{\beta}_q,\tilde{\beta}_q]$, there exists  $\ell_q(\beta)$ such that
\begin{align*}
    \lim_{N\to \infty} N^{-1} \log Z_N=     \lim_{N\to \infty} N^{-1} \log \mathbb E[Z_N] =\ell_p(\beta)\qquad \text{ a.s.}
\end{align*}
Moreover, $\ell_p(\beta)$ is differentiable over $(-\tilde{\beta}_q,\tilde{\beta}_q)$. 
\end{theorem}
Our main result is the following fluctuation resutls:
\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:partition-clt}
{
}
There exists $\beta_q\in (0,\tilde{\beta}_q]$ such that for any $\beta\in (-\beta_q,\beta_q)$,
 \begin{equation}\label{eq:scaled-relative-clt}
  N^{q/2-1}\left(Y_N(\beta)-1\right)
  \Longrightarrow
  \,\N\left(0,\frac{q!}{2}\beta^2(\ell_q'(\beta))^2\right).
\end{equation}
Moreover,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:standardized-clt}
  \frac{Z_N(\beta)-\E Z_N(\beta)}
  {\sqrt{\Var Z_N(\beta)}}
  \Longrightarrow\N(0,1).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:free-energy-clt}
Under the assumptions of Theorem~\ref{thm:partition-clt},
\[
  N^{q/2-1}\left(
    \log Z_N(\beta)-\E\log Z_N(\beta)
  \right)
  \Longrightarrow
  \,\N\left(0,\frac{q!}{2}\,\beta^2(\ell_q'(\beta))^2\right).
\]
\end{corollary}

\subsection{A brief background and relation to earlier work}
The Sachdev–Ye–Kitaev (SYK) model is a quantum-mechanical model of \(N\) fermionic degrees of freedom with random, all-to-all interactions. It originates from the work of Sachdev and Ye on random quantum magnets in 1993. A related Majorana-fermion model was introduced by Kitaev in 2015 in the context of quantum holography and is now known as the SYK model. Subsequent work, notably by Maldacena and Stanford, clarified its large-\(N\) structure and its connections to quantum chaos and nearly two-dimensional gravity.

The SYK model is strongly interacting, yet it remains analytically tractable in the large-\(N\) limit. Depending on how the inverse temperature \(\beta = \beta_N\) scales as \(N \to \infty\), the model exhibits several distinct asymptotic regimes.

(i) Fixed-temperature large-\(N\) regime ($\beta:\text{ fixed}$):  This is the basic mathematical large-\(N\) limit of the SYK model. It suppresses finite-size and disorder fluctuations and provides a tractable framework for studying finite-temperature observables and the emergence of the lower-temperature physical regimes.

(ii) Conformal scaling regime \((1\ll \beta \ll N)\): The system is strongly interacting and approximately conformal, while finite-\(N\) effects remain small. In this regime, the SYK model realizes a non-Fermi liquid, in which conventional quasiparticle excitations are absent and the low-energy behavior is governed instead by collective scale-invariant dynamics.



(iii) Schwarzian crossover regime \((\beta \asymp N)\): Finite-\(N\) fluctuations become of order one, and the Schwarzian effective theory becomes important. This regime is closely related to the boundary dynamics of nearly-\(\mathrm{AdS}_2\) gravity and therefore provides a direct link between SYK and low-dimensional quantum gravity.

(iv) Quantum-Schwarzian regime \((N\ll \beta)\): Quantum fluctuations of the Schwarzian mode become strong, and finite-\(N\) effects are essential. In this regime, the SYK model probes the fully quantum dynamics of the same Schwarzian degree of freedom that appears in nearly-\(\mathrm{AdS}_2\) gravity.

(vi) Finite-size / discrete-spectrum regime ($\liminf_N \log (\beta_N)/N > 0$): Individual energy levels become relevant, and hence, Random matrix theory are applied.

It is quite rare for a strongly interacting quantum system to exhibit such a rich hierarchy of asymptotic regimes while still remaining analytically accessible. This combination makes the SYK model an important testing ground for both mathematics and physics. In particular, many physicists study these regimes in order to understand more precisely how strongly interacting quantum systems behave across different scales, and to identify which mathematical structures and physical mechanisms persist in more realistic models. In this sense, the SYK model serves as a relatively simple prototype from which one can develop tools and ideas for the study of more complicated quantum many-body systems, disordered systems, quantum chaos, and models of quantum gravity.

Rigorous results have recently identified the high-temperature annealed and
quenched free-energy limits \cite{GPSZ2026} and established complex
zero-freeness and second-moment estimates \cite{Zlokapa2026}.  The purpose of
this paper is to determine the leading distributional fluctuations in the
same high-temperature regime. 


Feng, Tian, and Wei \cite{FTW2018} proved a central limit theorem for linear
eigenvalue statistics of the Gaussian SYK Hamiltonian.  Their theorem treats
a fixed test function $f$ under regularity assumptions on $f$ and $f'$.
In the normalization used in that paper, however, the partition function
corresponds to
\[
  f_{N,\beta}(x)=e^{-\beta\tau_Nx},
  \qquad \tau_N\asymp\sqrt N,
\]
so both the test function and its derivative vary with $N$.  The
fixed-test-function theorem therefore does not apply directly to
$Z_N(\beta)$.


\subsection{Outline of the proof}


By applying the chaos expansion, since Clifford sign-gauge symmetry eliminates the first Gaussian chaos and
identifies the second chaos exactly, we have the following:
\[
  Y_N(\beta)-1=
  A_N(\beta)Q_N+\mathcal R_N(\beta),
  \]
  where
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-N-FTW-coefficient}
          A_N(\beta)=\frac{\beta}{2}\partial_\beta\log F_N(\beta),
    \qquad
    Q_N=\frac1{|\mathcal I_N|}\sum_I(g_I^2-1).
    \end{equation}
Here $\mathcal R_N$ is the sum of the Gaussian chaoses of $Y_N$ of orders at least
three.  Thus the leading random term is a normalized sum of $\mathcal I_N$
independent centered variables.  The main task is to prove
\[
  \norm{\mathcal R_N(\beta)}_2^2=o(N^{-(q-2)}).
\]

{
Indeed, a Duhamel derivative bound and the sign-gauge
parity rule give
\begin{equation*}
  \sum_{r\geq3}\lVert P_r Y_N(\beta)\rVert_2^2
  \leq
  \sum_{r\geq3}(\beta\sigma_N)^{2r}
  \sum_{\substack{|\alpha|=r\\ \alpha\ \mathrm{even}}}
  \frac{1}{\alpha!}.
\end{equation*}
Using a combinatorial argument, we have that the entire higher-order sum is
bounded by  $O(N^{-3(q/2-1)})=o(N^{-(q-2)})$.
}

Moreover, the annealed free-energy input and convexity imply
\[
  \frac{A_N(\beta)}N
  \longrightarrow \frac{\beta}{2}\ell_q'(\beta).
\]
Since $M_N\sim N^q/q!$, the classical central limit theorem for $Q_N$, the
remainder estimate  gives the claimed limit for
$Y_N$.

\section{Gaussian-chaos decomposition}
\label{sec:chaos-decomposition}
  We use the probabilists' Hermite polynomials
\begin{equation*}
  H_\alpha(G)=\prod_{I\in\calI_N}{\rm He}_{\alpha_I}(g_I),
  \qquad
  \E[H_\alpha H_\eta]
  =\alpha!\,\mathbf 1_{\{\alpha=\eta\}},
\end{equation*}
where $\alpha!=\prod_I\alpha_I!$.  Since the $H_\alpha$ form a complete
orthogonal basis of $L^2(\gamma_{|\mathcal I_N|})$, where
$\gamma_{|\mathcal I_N|}$ denotes standard Gaussian measure on
$\mathbb R^{|\mathcal I_N|}$, we have
\begin{align}\label{eq: chaos}
Z_N(\beta:G) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \sum_{\alpha:|\alpha|=k} \frac{\mathbb E[Z_N(\beta:G')H_\alpha(G')]}{\alpha!} H_\alpha(G),    
\end{align}
where the second sum runs over all multi-index $\alpha$ such that $\sum_{I} \alpha_I = k$, and the expectation is taken over $G'$ independent copy of $G.$  Let $P_r$ denote the orthogonal projection in
$L^2(\gamma_{|\mathcal I_N|})$ onto the $r$-th homogeneous Gaussian chaos:
Then, we can write
\[
  P_r Z_N(\beta;G)
  =\sum_{|\alpha|=r}
  \frac{\mathbb E[Z_N(\beta;G')H_\alpha(G')]}{\alpha!}H_\alpha(G).
\]
Since $F_N(\beta)$ is deterministic, we also have
\[
  P_rY_N(\beta)=\frac{P_rZ_N(\beta)}{F_N(\beta)}.
\]


By the same orthogonality and Lemma~\ref{lem:Gaussian-Hermite-IBP}, for every
sufficiently regular random variable $X=X(G)$,
\begin{equation*}
  \norm{P_rX}_2^2
  =
  \sum_{|\alpha|=r}
  \frac{|\E\,\partial_g^\alpha X|^2}{\alpha!}.
\end{equation*}


Recall $A_N(\beta):=\frac{\beta}{2}\,\partial_\beta\log F_N(\beta)$ from \eqref{eq:finite-N-FTW-coefficient}.
 The next lemma proves the exact radial decomposition
\begin{equation*}
  Y_N(\beta)-1=A_N(\beta)Q_N+\mathcal R_N(\beta),
  \qquad
  \mathcal R_N=\sum_{r\geq3}P_r Y_N.
\end{equation*}


\subsection{Exact finite-size radial projection}

Call a multi-index $\alpha$ \emph{even} if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:even-multi-index}
  \sum_{I\ni x}\alpha_I\equiv0\pmod2
  \qquad\text{for every }x\in[N].
\end{equation}
Otherwise, we call $\alpha$ odd. 
\begin{lemma}\label{lem: parity rule lemma}
    For $s=(s_1,\ldots,s_N)\in\{\pm1\}^N$, define
$\chi_s(I)=\prod_{x\in I}s_x$.  We have 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sign-gauge-invariance}
  Z_N(\beta;G)=Z_N\bigl(\beta;(\chi_s(I)g_I)_I\bigr).
\end{equation}

    In particular, if $\alpha$ is odd, then
    \[
      \mathbb E[Z_N(\beta;G)H_\alpha(G)]=0.
    \]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
To make the Clifford symmetry explicit, let
\[
  \Gamma
  := 
  \psi_1\cdots\psi_N.
\]
Note that $\Gamma^*\Gamma$ is identity map, so $\Gamma$ is unitary. Since $N$ is even, moving $\psi_j$ through the other $N-1$ Majorana
generators produces the sign $(-1)^{N-1}=-1$.  Hence
\[
  \Gamma\psi_j\Gamma^*=-\psi_j
  \qquad (j\in[N]).
\]
For each $x\in[N]$, put $U_x=\Gamma\psi_x$.  This is unitary, and conjugation
by $U_x$ is the composition of conjugation by $\psi_x$ and conjugation by
$\Gamma$.  The Clifford relations give
\[
  \psi_x\psi_j\psi_x
  =
  \begin{cases}
    \psi_x,&j=x,\\
    -\psi_j,&j\neq x.
  \end{cases}
\]
Combining the last two displays, we obtain
\[
  U_x\psi_jU_x^*
  =
  \begin{cases}
    -\psi_x,&j=x,\\
    \psi_j,&j\neq x.
  \end{cases}
\]
Thus conjugation by $U_x$ changes the sign of precisely the generator
$\psi_x$.

Let $S_s=\{x\in[N]:s_x=-1\}$ and let $U_s$ be the product of the unitaries
$U_x$ over $x\in S_s$, in any fixed order.  Successively applying the
preceding conjugation rule yields
\[
  U_s\psi_jU_s^*=s_j\psi_j
  \qquad (j\in[N]).
\]
If $I=\{i_1<\cdots<i_q\}$, then conjugation is multiplicative, so the
definition $\psi_I=\mathrm i^{q/2}\psi_{i_1}\cdots\psi_{i_q}$ gives
\begin{align*}
  U_s\psi_IU_s^*
  &=
  \mathrm i^{q/2}
  (U_s\psi_{i_1}U_s^*)\cdots
  (U_s\psi_{i_q}U_s^*)\\
  &=
  \left(\prod_{x\in I}s_x\right)\psi_I
  =\chi_s(I)\psi_I.
\end{align*}
Writing $g^{(s)}=(\chi_s(I)g_I)_I$, we therefore have
\begin{align*}
  H_N(g^{(s)})
  &=\sigma_N\sum_{I\in\calI_N}\chi_s(I)g_I\psi_I\\
  &=\sigma_N\sum_{I\in\calI_N}g_IU_s\psi_IU_s^*
    =U_sH_N(G)U_s^*.
\end{align*}
The power-series definition of the exponential then implies
\[
  e^{-\beta H_N(g^{(s)})}
  =U_se^{-\beta H_N(G)}U_s^*.
\]
Finally, the normalized trace is invariant under unitary conjugation.
Consequently
\[
  Z_N(\beta;g^{(s)})
  =\tr\!\left(U_se^{-\beta H_N(G)}U_s^*\right)
  =\tr e^{-\beta H_N(G)}
  =Z_N(\beta;G),
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:sign-gauge-invariance}.

Assume that $\alpha$ is odd, i.e. we can take $x$ such that
$\sum_{I:x\in I}\alpha_I$ is odd.  We take $s$ such that $s_y=1$ for
$y\neq x$ and $s_x=-1$.  Then,
 \[
  H_\alpha((\chi_s(I)g_I)_I)
  =
  \left(
    \prod_{x=1}^N
    s_x^{\sum_{I\ni x}\alpha_I}
  \right)H_\alpha(G) = -H_\alpha(G).
\]
Hence, we have 
\begin{align*}
    \mathbb E[Z_N(G') H_\alpha(G')] =     \mathbb E[Z_N(\chi_s G') H_\alpha(\chi_s G')] = -
    \mathbb E[Z_N(G') H_\alpha(G')], 
\end{align*}
which gives the second claim. 
\end{proof}

\subsection{Gaussian integration by parts}
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:Gaussian-Hermite-IBP}
Let $f:\mathbb R^{|\mathcal I_N|}\to\mathbb R$ be of class $C^{|\alpha|}$,
and suppose that $f$ and its mixed partial derivatives up to order $\alpha$
have at most exponential growth in $\norm{G}$.  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Gaussian-Hermite-IBP}
  \mathbb E\!\left[H_\alpha(G)f(G)\right]
  =\mathbb E\!\left[\partial_g^\alpha f(G)\right].
\end{equation}
In particular, \eqref{eq:Gaussian-Hermite-IBP} holds for
$f(G)=Z_N(\beta;G)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Recall that the one-dimensional integration-by-parts identity yields 
\[
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\operatorname{He}_k(g)f(g)\right] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[f^{(k)}(g)\right], \qquad g\sim N(0,1),
\]
for every function $f\in C^k(\mathbb R)$ satisfying the stated growth
condition.  Applying this identity successively to the independent Gaussian
coordinates $(g_I)_{I\in\mathcal I_N}$ and using
\[
H_\alpha(G)
=\prod_{I\in\mathcal I_N}\operatorname{He}_{\alpha_I}(g_I),
\]
gives
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
H_\alpha(G)f(G)
\right]
&=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\left(
\prod_{I\in\mathcal I_N}
\operatorname{He}_{\alpha_I}(g_I)
\right)
f(G)
\right] \\
&=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\left(
\prod_{I\in\mathcal I_N}
\partial_{g_I}^{\alpha_I}
\right)
f(G)
\right] \\
&=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\partial_g^\alpha f(G)
\right].
\end{align*}

The growth assumption justifies all integrations by parts.  Finally, for fixed
$N$ and $\beta$, the partition function $Z_N(\beta;G)$ is a smooth function
of finitely many Gaussian coordinates, and all of its derivatives have at
most exponential growth in $\norm{G}$.  Thus it satisfies the assumption.
\end{proof}
\subsection{First and second moment}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:exact-second-chaos}
For every real $\beta$,
\[
  P_1Y_N(\beta)=0
\]
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:P2Y-exact}
  P_2 Y_N(\beta)
  =
  \frac{\beta\,\partial_\beta\log F_N(\beta)}{2M_N}
  \sum_{I\in\calI_N}(g_I^2-1).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  This parity rule above kills
 the first chaos.  In the second chaos it also kills every off-diagonal
monomial $g_Ig_J$, since the parity condition for such a monomial forces
$I=J$.
Permutation symmetry shows that the remaining diagonal coefficients agree.
Consequently
\[
  P_2Z_N(\beta)=b_N(\beta)\sum_I(g_I^2-1)
\]
for a deterministic scalar $b_N$.

It remains to compute the coefficient.  For each fixed $I$, the
probabilists' second Hermite polynomial is $g_I^2-1$, with
\[
  \E(g_I^2-1)^2=2.
\]
Since $P_2$ is an orthogonal projection and the variables $g_I$ are
independent, taking the inner product of the preceding formula for $P_2Z_N$
with $g_I^2-1$ gives
\[
  2b_N(\beta)
  =\E\bigl[Z_N(\beta)(g_I^2-1)\bigr].
\]
Applying Gaussian integration by parts twice, conditionally on all
coordinates other than $g_I$, yields
\[
  \E\bigl[Z_N(\beta)(g_I^2-1)\bigr]
  =\E\,g_I \partial_{g_I} Z_N(\beta).
\]
Summing over the $\mathcal I_N$ choices of $I$ therefore gives the first equality
below.  A further conditional Gaussian integration by parts gives the second:
\begin{align*}
  2M_Nb_N(\beta)
  & =\sum_I\E\bigl[g_I\partial_{g_I}Z_N(\beta)\bigr]\\
  &=\beta\,\partial_\beta\E Z_N(\beta)
    =\beta F_N'(\beta).
\end{align*}

The last equality follows pointwise from the exact scaling identity
\[
  Z_N(\beta;tG)
  =\tr e^{-\beta H_N(tG)}
  =\tr e^{-t\beta H_N(G)}
  =Z_N(t\beta;G),
\]
where we used $H_N(tG)=tH_N(G)$.  Differentiating at $t=1$ gives
\[
  \sum_Ig_I\partial_{g_I}Z_N(\beta;G)
  =\beta\,\partial_\beta Z_N(\beta;G).
\]
Since $F_N(\beta)$ is deterministic, division by $F_N(\beta)$ proves
\eqref{eq:P2Y-exact}.
\end{proof}

{
\subsection{Direct Rademacher control of all higher chaoses}
\label{sec:Rademacher-remainder}
}

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:Z-derivative-bound}
For real $\beta$, every multi-index $\alpha$ of total order $r$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Z-derivative-bound}
  \left|\partial_g^\alpha Z_N(\beta;G)\right|
  \leq (|\beta|\sigma_N)^r Z_N(\beta;G).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The case $r=0$ is immediate, so assume $r\geq1$.  Write $H_N=H_N(G)$ for
brevity.  We first derive the equality
\begin{align}
	&\partial_{g_{I_r}}\cdots\partial_{g_{I_1}}Z_N(\beta;G)
	\notag\\
	&\quad=(-\beta\sigma_N)^r
	\sum_{\pi\in S_r}\int_{\Delta_r}
	\tr\!\left(
	e^{-t_0\beta H_N}\psi_{I_{\pi(1)}}e^{-t_1\beta H_N}
	\cdots
	\psi_{I_{\pi(r)}}e^{-t_r\beta H_N}
	\right)\,\mathrm dt_1\cdots\mathrm dt_r
	\label{eq:iterated-Duhamel-formula}
\end{align}
where
\[\Delta_r=\left\{(t_j)_{j=1}^r: t_j\ge0,\quad \sum_{j=1}^r t_j\le 1\right\},\qquad t_0=1-\sum_{i=1}^r t_j. \]
For a differentiable matrix-valued function $A(\theta)$, the one-variable
Duhamel formula is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:one-variable-Duhamel-formula}
  \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\theta}e^{A(\theta)}
  =\int_0^1
    e^{sA(\theta)}A'(\theta)e^{(1-s)A(\theta)}\,\mathrm ds.
\end{equation}
By the definition of $H_N$ in Subsection~\ref{subsec:SYK-model},
\[
  \partial_{g_I}H_N=\sigma_N\psi_I.
\]
Applying \eqref{eq:one-variable-Duhamel-formula} to
$A=-t\beta H_N$ gives, for $t\geq0$,
\begin{align}
  \partial_{g_I}e^{-t\beta H_N}
  &=-t\beta\sigma_N\int_0^1
    e^{-st\beta H_N}\psi_I
    e^{-(1-s)t\beta H_N}\,\mathrm ds \notag\\
  &=-\beta\sigma_N\int_0^t
    e^{-u\beta H_N}\psi_I
    e^{-(t-u)\beta H_N}\,\mathrm du.
  \label{eq:Duhamel-interval-splitting}
\end{align}
Here, \eqref{eq:Duhamel-interval-splitting} with $t=1$ yields
\eqref{eq:iterated-Duhamel-formula} for $r=1$.  Suppose inductively that the
formula holds at order $r-1$.  Then
\begin{align}
	&\partial_{g_{I_r}}\cdots\partial_{g_{I_1}}Z_N(\beta;G)
	\notag\\
	&\quad=(-\beta\sigma_N)^{r-1}
	\sum_{\pi\in S_{r-1}}\int_{\Delta_{r-1}}\partial_{g_{I_r}}
	\tr\!\left(
	e^{-t_0\beta H_N}\psi_{I_{\pi(1)}}e^{-t_1\beta H_N}
	\cdots
	\psi_{I_{\pi(r-1)}}e^{-t_{r-1}\beta H_N}
	\right)\,\mathrm dt_1\cdots\mathrm dt_{r-1}.
	\label{eq:iterated-Duhamel-formula-induction}
\end{align}
For fixed $\pi\in S_{r-1}$ and $j\in\{1,\ldots,r\}$, differentiating the
factor $e^{-t_{j-1}\beta H_N}$ replaces it, by
\eqref{eq:Duhamel-interval-splitting}, with
\[
  -\beta\sigma_N\int_0^{t_{j-1}}
  e^{-u\beta H_N}\psi_{I_r}
  e^{-(t_{j-1}-u)\beta H_N}\,\mathrm du.
\]
Splitting $t_{j-1}$ into $u$ and $t_{j-1}-u$ and relabeling the resulting
simplex variables, the corresponding term in the Leibniz rule becomes
\begin{align*}
  -\beta\sigma_N\int_{\Delta_r}
  \tr\!\left(
    e^{-t_0\beta H_N}\psi_{I_{\sigma_{\pi,j}(1)}}e^{-t_1\beta H_N}
    \cdots
    \psi_{I_{\sigma_{\pi,j}(r)}}e^{-t_r\beta H_N}
  \right)\,\mathrm dt_1\cdots\mathrm dt_r,
\end{align*}
where
\[\sigma_{\pi,j}(k)=\begin{cases}
	\pi(k) & (k<j)\\
	r &(k=j)\\
	\pi(k-1) & (k>j)
\end{cases}. \]
Since $(\pi,j)\mapsto\sigma_{\pi,j}$ is a bijection from
$S_{r-1}\times\{1,\ldots,r\}$ onto $S_r$, summing the Leibniz-rule terms in
\eqref{eq:iterated-Duhamel-formula-induction} proves
\eqref{eq:iterated-Duhamel-formula}.

It remains to estimate each integrand in
\eqref{eq:iterated-Duhamel-formula}.  Here and below the Schatten norms are
defined using the normalized trace:
\[
  \norm{A}_p=(\tr|A|^p)^{1/p},
  \qquad
  \norm{A}_\infty=\norm{A}.
\]
For $t_j>0$,
\[
  \norm{e^{-t_j\beta H_N}}_{1/t_j}
  =
  \bigl(\tr e^{-\beta H_N}\bigr)^{t_j},
\]
with the usual $p=\infty$ interpretation when $t_j=0$.  By
Subsection~\ref{subsec:SYK-model}, $\norm{\psi_I}_\infty=1$.
Noncommutative H\"older, with Schatten exponents $1/t_j$, therefore gives,
for every $\pi\in S_r$ and
$t\in\Delta_r$,
\begin{align}
  &\left|
  \tr\!\left(
    e^{-t_0\beta H_N}\psi_{I_{\pi(1)}}e^{-t_1\beta H_N}
    \cdots
    \psi_{I_{\pi(r)}}e^{-t_r\beta H_N}
  \right)
  \right| \notag\\
  &\quad\leq
  \prod_{j=0}^r\norm{e^{-t_j\beta H_N}}_{1/t_j}
  \prod_{k=1}^r\norm{\psi_{I_{\pi(k)}}}_\infty \notag\\
  &\quad\leq
  \prod_{j=0}^r
  \left(\tr e^{-\beta H_N}\right)^{t_j}
  =\tr e^{-\beta H_N}
  =Z_N(\beta;G),
  \label{eq:Duhamel-integrand-Holder-bound}
\end{align}
where we used $\sum_{j=0}^rt_j=1$.  
Combining \eqref{eq:iterated-Duhamel-formula} and 
\eqref{eq:Duhamel-integrand-Holder-bound}, we obtain
\begin{align*}
 \left|
  \partial_{g_{I_r}}\cdots\partial_{g_{I_1}}Z_N(\beta;G)
  \right|
  &\leq
  (|\beta|\sigma_N)^r
  \sum_{\pi\in S_r}\int_{\Delta_r}Z_N(\beta;G)
  \,\mathrm dt_1\cdots\mathrm dt_r\\
  &=
  (|\beta|\sigma_N)^rZ_N(\beta;G)|S_r|\int_{\Delta_r}\mathrm dt_1\cdots\mathrm dt_r\\
  &=
  (|\beta|\sigma_N)^rZ_N(\beta;G),
\end{align*}
which proves \eqref{eq:Z-derivative-bound}.
\end{proof}

Recall that Lemma~\ref{lem: parity rule lemma} shows that
only even multi-indices have nonzero Hermite coefficients.

Let $(\varepsilon_x)_{x=1}^N$ be independent Rademacher variables on an
auxiliary probability space, and write $\E_\varepsilon$ for expectation on
this space.  Set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Rademacher-chaos-definition}
  S_{N,q}
  =\sum_{I\in\calI_N}\varepsilon_I,
  \qquad
  \varepsilon_I=\prod_{x\in I}\varepsilon_x.
\end{equation}
Thus $S_{N,q}$ is the degree-$q$ elementary symmetric polynomial in the
Rademacher signs.

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:Rademacher-encoding}
For every integer $r\geq0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:even-sum-Rademacher-moment}
  \sum_{\substack{|\alpha|=r\\ \alpha\ \mathrm{even}}}
  \frac1{\alpha!}
  =\frac1{r!}\E_\varepsilon\!\left[S_{N,q}^r\right].
\end{equation}
Moreover, there are constants $c_q>0$ and $C_q<\infty$, depending only on
$q$, such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Rademacher-exponential-moment}
  \E_\varepsilon\!\left[
    |S_{N,q}|^3e^{t|S_{N,q}|}
  \right]
  \leq C_qN^{3q/2}
\end{equation}
for every $N$ and every $0\leq t\leq c_qN^{-(q-1)}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Starting from the definition \eqref{eq:Rademacher-chaos-definition} and
applying the multinomial formula gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Rademacher-multinomial-expansion}
  \frac1{r!}\E_\varepsilon\!\left[S_{N,q}^r\right]
  =\frac1{r!}\E_\varepsilon\!\left[
    \sum_{|\alpha|=r}r!
    \prod_{I\in\calI_N}\frac{\varepsilon_I^{\alpha_I}}{\alpha_I!}
  \right]
  =
  \sum_{|\alpha|=r}\frac1{\alpha!}
  \E_\varepsilon\!\left[
    \prod_{I\in\calI_N}\varepsilon_I^{\alpha_I}
  \right].
\end{equation}
For a fixed $\alpha$, the product inside the expectation is
\begin{equation*}
  \prod_{x=1}^N
  \varepsilon_x^{\sum_{I: x\in I}\alpha_I}.
\end{equation*}
By independence of the Rademacher variables and the definition of an even
multi-index in \eqref{eq:even-multi-index}, its expectation is one if
$\alpha$ is even and zero otherwise.  Substitution into
\eqref{eq:Rademacher-multinomial-expansion} proves
\eqref{eq:even-sum-Rademacher-moment}.

We next prove a deterministic bound for $S_{N,q}$.  For every $N\geq1$
and every integer $m\geq0$, let $e_m^{(N)}$ denote the $m$th elementary
symmetric polynomial in $N$ variables, that is,
\begin{equation*}
  e_m^{(N)}(z_1,\ldots,z_N)
  :=\sum_{\substack{I\subset[N]\\ |I|=m}}
  \prod_{x\in I}z_x,
  \qquad e_0^{(N)}(z_1,\ldots,z_N)=1.
\end{equation*}
For the rest of the proof, we use the abbreviation
\[
  e_m:=e_m^{(N)}(\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_N).
\]
In particular, $e_1=\sum_{x=1}^N\varepsilon_x$, and
\eqref{eq:Rademacher-chaos-definition} gives $S_{N,q}=e_q$.
Newton's identities state that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Newton-identities}
  m e_m
  =\sum_{j=1}^m(-1)^{j-1}e_{m-j}p_j,
\end{equation}
where $p_j:=\sum_{x=1}^N\varepsilon_x^j$.  Since
$\varepsilon_x\in\{\pm1\}$, we have
\begin{equation*}
  p_j
  =
  \begin{cases}
    e_1,&j\text{ odd},\\
    N,&j\text{ even}.
  \end{cases}
\end{equation*}
An induction on $m$, using \eqref{eq:Newton-identities}, shows that there is a constant $C_m<\infty$ such that
\begin{equation*}
	|e_m|
	\le C_m\left(N^{m/2}+|e_1|^m\right).
\end{equation*}
Indeed, suppose that the estimate is known below order $m$ and set
\[
X_N:=\frac{e_1}{\sqrt N}.
\]
For every $j\ge1$,
\[
|p_j|\le N^{j/2}(1+|X_N|^j).
\]
Hence, with $C_0=1$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
	|e_{m-j}p_j|
	&\le
	C_{m-j}N^{m/2}
	(1+|X_N|^{m-j})(1+|X_N|^j)\\
	&\le
	2C_{m-j}N^{m/2}(1+|X_N|^m),
\end{aligned}
\]
where we used $1+|X|^m-|X|^{m-j}-|X|^j
=(1-|X|^j)(1-|X|^{m-j})\ge0.$
Substitution into \eqref{eq:Newton-identities} proves the induction step.
Taking $m=q$ and using \eqref{eq:Rademacher-chaos-definition}, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Rademacher-chaos-deterministic-bound}
  |S_{N,q}|
  \leq C_q\left(N^{q/2}+|e_1|^q\right).
\end{equation}

Since $q\geq4$ by Subsection~\ref{subsec:SYK-model} and
$|X_N|\leq\sqrt N$,
\eqref{eq:Rademacher-chaos-deterministic-bound} implies that,
for $0\leq t\leq c_qN^{-(q-1)}$,
\begin{align}
  t|S_{N,q}|
  &\leq C_qc_qN^{-(q/2-1)}\left(1+|X_N|^q\right) \notag\\
  &\leq C_qc_q\left(1+|X_N|^2\right).
  \label{eq:Rademacher-exponent-reduction}
\end{align}
Indeed,
\begin{equation*}
  N^{-(q/2-1)}|X_N|^q
  =|X_N|^2
  \left(\frac{|X_N|}{\sqrt N}\right)^{q-2}
  \leq |X_N|^2.
\end{equation*}
Choose $c_q$ small enough that the last member of
\eqref{eq:Rademacher-exponent-reduction} is bounded by
$(1+|X_N|^2)/4$.

Hoeffding's inequality gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Hoeffding-Rademacher-sum}
  \mathbb P_\varepsilon(|X_N|\geq u)
  \leq2e^{-u^2/2}
  \qquad (u\geq0).
\end{equation}
Consequently, integration of the tail in
\eqref{eq:Hoeffding-Rademacher-sum} yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uniform-Rademacher-integrability}
  \sup_{N\geq1}
  \E_\varepsilon\!\left[
    \left(1+|X_N|^{3q}\right)e^{|X_N|^2/4}
  \right]
  <\infty.
\end{equation}
Finally, \eqref{eq:Rademacher-chaos-deterministic-bound},
\eqref{eq:Rademacher-exponent-reduction}, and
\eqref{eq:uniform-Rademacher-integrability} give
\begin{align*}
  \E_\varepsilon\!\left[
    |S_{N,q}|^3e^{t|S_{N,q}|}
  \right]
  &\leq
  C_qN^{3q/2}
  \E_\varepsilon\!\left[
    \left(1+|X_N|^{3q}\right)
    e^{(1+|X_N|^2)/4}
  \right] \\
  &\leq C_qN^{3q/2},
\end{align*}
which proves \eqref{eq:Rademacher-exponential-moment}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition} \label{prop:higher-chaos-closure}
There exists $\beta_{\rm Rad}=\beta_{\rm Rad}(q)>0$ such that, for every
fixed real $\beta$ with $|\beta|<\beta_{\rm Rad}$,
\begin{equation*}
  \sum_{r\geq3}\norm{P_r Y_N(\beta)}_2^2
  =O\!\left(N^{-3(q/2-1)}\right)
  =o(N^{2-q}).
\end{equation*}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Put
\begin{equation*}
  t_N=\beta^2\sigma_N^2
  =\beta^2(q-1)!N^{-(q-1)}.
\end{equation*}
By the Hermite expansion \eqref{eq: chaos} and its orthogonality relation,
the squared norm of the $r$th projection is the sum of the squared Hermite
coefficients.  Lemma~\ref{lem: parity rule lemma} eliminates every odd
multi-index, while Lemma~\ref{lem:Gaussian-Hermite-IBP} replaces each
remaining Hermite coefficient by the corresponding expected derivative.
Consequently, Lemma~\ref{lem:Z-derivative-bound} and $F_N(\beta)>0$ give
\begin{align}
  \norm{P_r Y_N(\beta)}_2^2
  &=\frac1{F_N(\beta)^2}
    \sum_{\substack{|\alpha|=r\\ \alpha\ \mathrm{even}}}
    \frac1{\alpha!}
    \left|\E\partial_g^\alpha Z_N(\beta)\right|^2 \notag\\
  &\leq t_N^r
    \sum_{\substack{|\alpha|=r\\ \alpha\ \mathrm{even}}}
    \frac1{\alpha!}.
  \label{eq:Hermite-Rademacher-bound}
\end{align}
By the first assertion of Lemma~\ref{lem:Rademacher-encoding}, namely
\eqref{eq:even-sum-Rademacher-moment}, summing
\eqref{eq:Hermite-Rademacher-bound} over all $r\geq3$ yields
\begin{align}
  \sum_{r\geq3}\norm{P_r Y_N(\beta)}_2^2
  &\leq
  \sum_{r\geq3}\frac{t_N^r}{r!}
  \E_\varepsilon\!\left[S_{N,q}^r\right] \notag\\
  &\leq
  \E_\varepsilon\!\left[
    \sum_{r\geq3}\frac{(t_N|S_{N,q}|)^r}{r!}
  \right] \notag\\
  &\leq
  \frac{t_N^3}{6}
  \E_\varepsilon\!\left[
    |S_{N,q}|^3e^{t_N|S_{N,q}|}
  \right].
  \label{eq:all-higher-chaoses-at-once}
\end{align}
Here the second inequality uses
$|\E_\varepsilon[S_{N,q}^r]|\leq
\E_\varepsilon[|S_{N,q}|^r]$, and the last one uses
$\sum_{r\geq3}u^r/r!\leq u^3e^u/6$ for $u\geq0$.
Choose
\begin{equation*}
  \beta_{\rm Rad}
  =\sqrt{\frac{c_q}{(q-1)!}},
\end{equation*}
where $c_q$ is the constant in Lemma~\ref{lem:Rademacher-encoding}.  If
$|\beta|<\beta_{\rm Rad}$, then
$t_N\leq c_qN^{-(q-1)}$.  Therefore the second assertion of
Lemma~\ref{lem:Rademacher-encoding}, namely
\eqref{eq:Rademacher-exponential-moment}, together with
\eqref{eq:all-higher-chaoses-at-once} imply
\begin{align*}
  \sum_{r\geq3}\norm{P_r Y_N(\beta)}_2^2
  &\leq C_{q,\beta}
  N^{-3(q-1)}N^{3q/2} \\
  &=C_{q,\beta}N^{-3(q/2-1)}.
\end{align*}
Since $q\geq4$, we have $3(q/2-1)>q-2$, which completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Annealed free energy and coefficient asymptotics}

To identify the deterministic coefficient $A_N(\beta)$ in
\eqref{eq:finite-N-FTW-coefficient}, we use the annealed free-energy limit
from \cite{GPSZ2026}.  The required convergence of derivatives follows from
convexity and is not an additional input.

\begin{lemma} \label{lem:convex-derivatives}
Let $I\subset\mathbb R$ be open.  Suppose that $h_N:I\to\mathbb R$ are finite,
differentiable, convex functions and $h_N(x)\to h(x)$ pointwise, where
$h\in C^1(I)$.  Then $h_N\to h$ and $h_N'\to h'$ locally uniformly on $I$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix a compact interval $[a,b]\subset I$ and choose $u<a<b<v$ in $I$.
Monotonicity of secant slopes bounds every secant slope of $h_N$ on $[a,b]$
between quantities formed from $h_N(u),h_N(a),h_N(b),h_N(v)$.  These
endpoint values converge, so the functions $h_N$ are equi-Lipschitz on
$[a,b]$.  Pointwise convergence on a finite mesh, followed by mesh
refinement, gives uniform convergence there.

For $x\in I$ and sufficiently small $s>0$, convexity gives
\[
  \frac{h_N(x)-h_N(x-s)}s
  \leq h_N'(x)\leq
  \frac{h_N(x+s)-h_N(x)}s.
\]
First let $N\to\infty$ and then $s\downarrow0$ to obtain
$h_N'(x)\to h'(x)$.  Finally, $h_N'$ are nondecreasing.  On a fine partition
of $[a,b]$, monotonicity traps $h_N'(x)$ between its values at adjacent mesh
points, while uniform continuity traps $h'(x)$ between the corresponding
values of $h'$.  Pointwise convergence at the finitely many mesh points then
gives uniform convergence of the derivatives.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:annealed-free-energy}
There are $\beta_{\rm FE}=\beta_{\rm FE}(q)>0$ and an even real-analytic
function $\ell_q$ on $(-\beta_{\rm FE},\beta_{\rm FE})$ such that, locally
uniformly on this interval,
\begin{align}
  \frac1N\log F_N(\beta)&\longrightarrow\ell_q(\beta),
  \label{eq:annealed-free-energy-limit}\\
  \frac1N\partial_\beta\log F_N(\beta)&\longrightarrow\ell_q'(\beta).
  \label{eq:annealed-derivative-limit}
\end{align}
Moreover,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ell-small-beta}
  \ell_q(\beta)=\frac{\beta^2}{2q}+O(\beta^4),
  \qquad
  \ell_q'(\beta)=\frac\beta q+O(\beta^3).
\end{equation}
In paritcular, $\ell'(\beta)>0$, for every sufficiently
small real $\beta\neq0$.

\end{proposition}


\begin{proof}
The pointwise convergence in \eqref{eq:annealed-free-energy-limit} is Theorem
5 of \cite{GPSZ2026}, together with the Gaussian extension stated there.  That
extension invokes the Gaussian--Rademacher universality result
\cite{AGK2025}. 

We first record the normalization conversion.  The
Majoranas of \cite{GPSZ2026} satisfy
\[
  \psi_i^{\rm GPSZ}=2^{-1/2}\psi_i,
  \qquad
  H_N^{\rm GPSZ}=2^{-q/2}H_N.
\]
Set $u=2^{q/2}\beta$.  Gaussian symmetry gives
\[
  \E\tr e^{-\beta H_N}
  =
  \E\tr e^{+\beta H_N},
\]
and $uH_N^{\rm GPSZ}=\beta H_N$.  Thus the sign and normalization conventions
of \cite{GPSZ2026} give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:GPSZ-conversion}
  \frac1N\log F_N(\beta)=\frac{u}{N}f_{N,q}^{\rm GPSZ}(u),
\end{equation}
where $f_{N,q}^{\rm GPSZ}(u)=u^{-1}\log\E Z_N^{\rm GPSZ}(u)$.

We next extract the regularity and the first coefficient from the explicit
formula in that theorem.  In its notation,
\[
  \Phi_u(z)=\sum_{v\geq1}\mu_v(u)z^v,
  \qquad
  u f_q^{\rm GPSZ}(u)=\Phi_u(t_*(u))-1-\log t_*(u),
\]
where $t_*\partial_z\Phi_u(t_*)=1$.  A contributing hypertree has
$v=1+(q-1)s$ vertices and its coefficient contains
\[
  \rho(u)^{v-1},
  \qquad
  \rho(u)^{q-1}=\frac{(q-1)!u^2}{2^{q+1}}.
\]
More explicitly, $\mathcal T_v^{\rm sim}$ is empty unless
$v=1+(q-1)s$ for some $s\geq0$.  For $s\geq1$ we may therefore write
\[
  \mu_{1+(q-1)s}(u)=c_{q,s}u^{2s},
\]
where
\[
  c_{q,s}
  =
  \frac1{(1+(q-1)s)!}
  \left(\frac{(q-1)!}{2^{q+1}}\right)^s
  \sum_{T\in\mathcal T_{1+(q-1)s}^{\rm sim}}b_T.
\]
The bounds used in the proof of Proposition 3 of \cite{GPSZ2026} give
$|c_{q,s}|\leq C_q^s$.  Hence
\[
  \Phi_u(z)
  =
  z+\sum_{s\geq1}
  c_{q,s}u^{2s}z^{1+(q-1)s}
\]
converges normally for $(u^2,z)$ in a neighborhood of $(0,1)$.  Thus
$\Phi_u(z)$ is jointly analytic in $(u^2,z)$ there.  At
$u=0$, $\Phi_0(z)=z$ and $t_*(0)=1$; the derivative with respect to $t$ of
$t\partial_z\Phi_u(t)-1$ is $1$ at $(u,t)=(0,1)$.  The analytic implicit
function theorem makes $t_*$, and hence the limiting right-hand side, analytic
in $u^2$.

The first nontrivial term is the unique one-edge hypertree on $q$ vertices:
\[
  \Phi_u(z)=z+\frac{u^2}{q2^{q+1}}z^q+O(u^4).
\]
It follows successively that
\[
  t_*(u)=1-\frac{u^2}{2^{q+1}}+O(u^4),
  \qquad
  u f_q^{\rm GPSZ}(u)=\frac{u^2}{q2^{q+1}}+O(u^4).
\]
Using $u=2^{q/2}\beta$ in \eqref{eq:GPSZ-conversion} gives
\eqref{eq:ell-small-beta}.

It remains only to justify the two local-uniform assertions.  Let
$h_N=N^{-1}\log F_N$.  If
\[
  \overline\nu_N(B)=\E\,\tr\mathbf1_{\{H_N\in B\}},
\]
then $F_N(\beta)=\int e^{-\beta x}\overline\nu_N(\mathrm dx)$; hence $h_N$
is finite, differentiable, and convex.  {Differentiation is
justified by
$\norm{H_N(G)}\leq\sigma_N\sum_{I\in\calI_N}|g_I|$ and Gaussian
exponential integrability.}
The limiting function just obtained is $C^1$.  Lemma
\ref{lem:convex-derivatives} now proves both local-uniform convergences.
\end{proof}

By \eqref{eq:finite-N-FTW-coefficient} and
\eqref{eq:annealed-derivative-limit},
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{A_N(\beta)}N\longrightarrow
  \frac\beta2\ell_q'(\beta).

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:partition-clt}]
Let

\]
The classical i.i.d.\ central limit theorem gives
$S_N\Rightarrow\xi\sim\N(0,1)$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:exact-second-chaos},

  Y_N(\beta)-1

  \qquad

\begin{lemma}[Gaussian concentration]
  Write 
  \begin{equation*}
    W_N \coloneqq \log Y_N(\beta).
  \end{equation*}
  Then, for $\beta\neq0$ and any $\lambda, t > 0$,
  \begin{align}
    \mathbb P\!\left(W_N - \E W_N < -t \right)
    &\leq \exp\left(-\frac{qt^2}{2\beta^2}\right),
    \label{eq:centered W_N tail}\\
    \E\exp\!\left(\lambda(W_N - \E W_N)\right) 
    &\leq \exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda^2 \beta^2}{2q}\right).
    \label{eq:centered W_N exp}
  \end{align}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
%  Since $F_N(\beta)$ is deterministic, the derivatives of $W_N$ are the
%  derivatives of $\log Z_N(\beta)$.
To apply the Gaussian concentration
  inequality, we view $Z_N(\beta;G)$ as a function of
  $G = (g_I)_{I \in \calI_N}$ and establish the Lipschitz bound for $W_N$.
  Fix $I \in \calI_N$.
  By Duhamel's formula,
  we deduce that 
  \begin{align*}
    \partial_{g_I} Z_N 
    &= 
    \tr \partial_{g_I} e^{-\beta H_N(G)}\\
    &=
    -\beta     
    \tr \!\left( \partial_{g_I} H_N(G) \cdot e^{-\beta H_N(G)} \right)\\ 
    &=
    -\beta \sigma_N \tr\bigl( e^{-\beta H_N} \psi_I \bigr).
  \end{align*}
  Write $\langle \cdot \rangle_G$ 
  for the expectation with respect to the normalized Gibbs state,
  i.e.,
  \begin{equation*}
    \langle A \rangle_G
    \coloneqq 
    \frac{\tr(A e^{-\beta H_N(G)})}{Z_N(\beta;G)}.
  \end{equation*}
  Then we obtain 
  \begin{equation} \label{eq:GC1}
    \partial_{g_I} W_N = -\beta \sigma_N \langle \psi_I \rangle_G.
  \end{equation}
  Fix an arbitrary subset $S \subseteq [N]$ with $|S| = q-1$.
  Set $A_i \coloneqq \psi_{S \cup \{i\}}$ for each $i \notin S$.
  Note that, by the relation $\{\psi_i, \psi_j\} = 2\delta_{ij} \I$,
  it holds that   
  \begin{equation} \label{eq:A_i relation}
    A_i^2 = \I,
    \qquad 
    A_i A_j + A_j A_i = 0 \quad \text{if}\quad i \neq j.
  \end{equation}
  This yields 
  \begin{equation*}
    \left(\sum_{i \notin S} \langle A_i \rangle_G A_i\right)^2
    = 
    \sum_{i \notin S}  \langle A_i \rangle_G^2 A_i^2
    +
    \sum_{\substack{i,j \notin S}\\ i < j}
    \langle A_i \rangle_G \langle A_j \rangle_G
    (A_iA_j + A_j A_i) 
    = 
    \sum_{i \notin S}  \langle A_i \rangle_G^2 \I.
  \end{equation*}
  If we write $\|\cdot\|$ for the operator norm,
  then, by \eqref{eq:A_i relation},
  we obtain 
  \begin{equation*}
    \left\|\left(\sum_{i \notin S} \langle A_i \rangle_G A_i\right)^2\right\|
    = 
    \sum_{i \notin S}  \langle A_i \rangle_G^2.
  \end{equation*}
  Since $A_i$ is self-adjoint,
  it follows that 
  \begin{equation} \label{eq:GC2}
    \left\| \sum_{i \notin S} \langle A_i \rangle_G A_i \right\| 
    = \sqrt{\sum_{i \notin S}  \langle A_i \rangle_G^2}.
  \end{equation}
  On the other hand, H\"{o}lder's inequality for Schatten norms yields 
  \begin{equation} \label{eq:GC3}
    \left\langle \sum_{i \notin S} \langle A_i \rangle_G A_i \right\rangle_G 
    \leq
    \left\| \sum_{i \notin S} \langle A_i \rangle_G A_i \right\|.
  \end{equation}
  Since the left-hand side equals $\sum_{i \notin S} \langle A_i \rangle_G^2$,
  we deduce from \eqref{eq:GC2} and \eqref{eq:GC3} that 
  \begin{equation} \label{eq:GC4}
    \sum_{i \notin S} \langle A_i \rangle^2 \leq 1.
  \end{equation}
  For each $I \in \calI_N$,
  there exist exactly $q$ subsets $S \subseteq [N]$ satisfying $I = S \cup \{i\}$.
  Hence,
  \begin{equation*}
    \sum_{\substack{S \subseteq [N]\\ |S| = q-1}} 
    \sum_{i \notin S} 
    \langle \psi_{S \cup \{i\}}\rangle_G^2 
    = 
    q \sum_{I \in \calI_N} \langle \psi_I\rangle_G^2.
  \end{equation*} 
  This, combined with \eqref{eq:GC4}, yields 
  \begin{equation*}
    \sum_{I \in \calI_N} \langle \psi_I \rangle_G^2
    \leq 
    \frac{1}{q} 
    \sum_{\substack{S \subseteq [N]\\ |S| = q-1}} 1 
    = 
    \frac{1}{q} \binom{N}{q-1}
    \leq    
    \frac{N^{q-1}}{q!}.
  \end{equation*}
  From this and \eqref{eq:GC1}, we deduce that 
  \begin{equation*}
    \left\| \nabla W_N \right\|_2^2 
    \leq
    \frac{\beta^2 \sigma_N^2 N^{q-1}}{q!}
    = 
    \frac{\beta^2}{q},
  \end{equation*}
  where the last equality follows from the definition of $\sigma_N^2$.
  This implies that $W_N(G)$ is $|\beta|/\sqrt q$-Lipschitz as a function of $G = (g_I)_{I \in \calI_N}$.
  Hence, the Gaussian concentration inequality gives the desired result.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:free-energy-clt}]
The case $\beta=0$ is trivial, so assume $\beta\neq0$.
Put
\[
  a=\frac q2-1,\qquad
  W_N=\log Y_N(\beta).
\]
It then holds that 
\begin{align*}
  \log Z_N - \E\log Z_N 
  &=
  (\log Z_N - \log \E Z_N) + (\log \E Z_N - \E \log Z_N)\\
  &=
  W_N - \E W_N\\
  &= 
  \bigl(W_N-(Y_N(\beta)-1)\bigr)
  +(Y_N(\beta)-1)-\E W_N,
\end{align*}
which yields 
\begin{equation*}
  N^a(\log Z_N - \E \log Z_N)
  =
  N^a\bigl(W_N-(Y_N(\beta)-1)\bigr)
  +N^a(Y_N(\beta)-1)-N^a \E W_N.
\end{equation*}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:partition-clt},
we have 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:cor1}
    N^a(Y_N(\beta)-1)
    \Longrightarrow
    \sqrt{\frac{q!}{2}}\,\beta\ell_q'(\beta)\,\xi.
\end{equation}
Let $C_0 \in (0,\infty)$ be such that 
$|\log (1+x) - x| \leq C_0x^2$ for all $x \in [-1/2, 1/2]$.
Since $W_N = \log Y_N(\beta)=\log(1+(Y_N(\beta)-1))$,
it follows that 
\begin{equation*}
  \E \bigl[ 1 \wedge (N^a|W_N-(Y_N(\beta)-1)|) \bigr]
  \leq
  \E \bigl[ 1 \wedge (C_0 N^a|Y_N(\beta)-1|^2) \bigr]
  + 
  \mathbb P(|Y_N(\beta)-1| > 1/2).
\end{equation*}
By \eqref{eq:cor1},
$|Y_N(\beta)-1| \xrightarrow{\mathrm{p}} 0$ and
$(N^a |Y_N(\beta)-1|)_{N \geq 1}$ is tight.
Thus, 
the right-hand side of the above inequality converges to $0$ as $N \to \infty$.
Hence, $N^a(W_N-(Y_N(\beta)-1)) \xrightarrow{\mathrm{p}} 0$.
Therefore, it suffices to show that $\E W_N = o(N^{-a})$.
% To this end,
% we first establish a Lipschitz bound for $G\mapsto W_N(G)$.  
% Thus $W_N=\log(1+(Y_N-1))$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:partition-clt} gives
% We claim that
% \begin{equation}\label{eq:log-centering-negligible}
%   \E W_N=o(N^{-(q/2-1)}).
% \end{equation}
% Differentiation gives
% \[
%   \partial_{g_I}W_N
%   =-\beta\sigma_N\langle\psi_I\rangle_{\beta,g}.
% \]
% For each $(q-1)$-set $S$, the operators
% $(\psi_{S\cup\{i\}})_{i\notin S}$ are pairwise anticommuting Hermitian
% involutions.  If $A_1,\ldots,A_m$ are any such operators and
% $a_j=\langle A_j\rangle$ in a state, set $B=\sum_j a_jA_j$ and
% $s=\sum_j a_j^2$.  Pairwise anticommutation gives
% \[
%   B^2=s\I,
%   \qquad
%   s=\langle B\rangle\leq\norm{B}=\sqrt{s},
% \]
% and hence $s\leq1$.  Applying this observation for each $S$ and noting that
% every $I\in\calI_N$ is counted exactly $q$ times yields
% \[
%   \sum_{I\in\calI_N}|\langle\psi_I\rangle_{\beta,g}|^2
%   \leq \frac1q\binom N{q-1}.
% \]
% Consequently,
% \[
%   \sum_{I\in\calI_N}|\partial_{g_I}W_N|^2
%   \leq
%   \frac{\beta^2\sigma_N^2}{q}\binom N{q-1}
%   \leq\frac{\beta^2}{q}.
% \]
% Writing $\mu_N=\E W_N$, the Gaussian concentration inequality therefore
% implies, for every $\lambda\in\mathbb R$,
% \begin{equation}\label{eq:WN-subgaussian}
%   \E e^{\lambda(W_N-\mu_N)}
%   \leq \exp\left(\frac{\lambda^2\beta^2}{2q}\right).
% \end{equation}

We claim that 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:EWN range}
  -\frac{\beta^2}{2q}\leq \E W_N\leq0.
\end{equation}
Indeed, the upper bound follows from Jensen's inequality,
while the lower bound follows from \eqref{eq:centered W_N exp} and the identity $\E e^{W_N}=1$.
Let $K_\beta=\beta^2/(2q)$ and
$\delta_N \coloneqq (\E (Y_N(\beta)-1)^2)^{1/2}$.
By Theorem~\ref{thm:partition-clt},
\begin{equation} \label{eq:deltaN asymp}
  \delta_N^2 = \E (Y_N(\beta)-1)^2\asymp N^{-(q-2)}.
\end{equation}
Henceforth, we consider sufficiently large $N$ so that $\delta_N \leq e^{-1}$.
Since $e^{W_N}-1=Y_N(\beta)-1$,
Markov's inequality gives
\begin{equation} \label{eq:W_N tail1}
  \mathbb P(W_N<-t)
  \leq \frac{\delta_N^2}{(1-e^{-t})^2}
  \leq C \delta_N^2,
  \qquad 
  \forall t \geq 1.
\end{equation}
On the other hand, 
by $\E W_N \geq -K_\beta$ and \eqref{eq:centered W_N tail},
\begin{equation} \label{eq:W_N tail2}
  \mathbb P(W_N<-t)
  \leq
  \exp\left(-\frac{q(t-K_\beta)^2}{2\beta^2}\right),
  \qquad 
  \forall t>K_\beta.
\end{equation}
Set
\[
  T_N=\max\left\{
    1,\,
    K_\beta+\frac{2|\beta|}{\sqrt q}
    \sqrt{\log(1/\delta_N)}
  \right\}.
\]
By the layer-cake representation,
we have 
\[
  \E\bigl[|W_N|\,\mathbf1_{\{W_N<-1\}}\bigr]
  =
  \mathbb P(W_N<-1)+
  \int_1^\infty\mathbb P(W_N<-t)\,\mathrm dt.
\]
Applying \eqref{eq:W_N tail1} to the first term and the integral over $[1,T_N]$
and applying \eqref{eq:W_N tail2} to the integral over $(T_N, \infty)$,
we obtain
\begin{equation*}
  \E\bigl[|W_N|\,\mathbf1_{\{W_N<-1\}}\bigr]
  \leq     
  C T_N \delta_N^2
  +
  \int_{T_N}^\infty \exp\left(-\frac{q(t-K_\beta)^2}{2\beta^2}\right) \mathrm dt.
\end{equation*}
By the Gaussian tail bound $\int_a^\infty e^{-t^2} \mathrm dt \leq C e^{-a^2}$ for any $a \geq 0$,
the second term is bounded by $C \delta_N^2$. 
Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:WN-negative-tail}
  \E\bigl[|W_N|\,\mathbf1_{\{W_N<-1\}}\bigr]
  \leq C\delta_N^2\sqrt{\log(1/\delta_N)}.
\end{equation}
Now set $h(w)=e^w-1-w$.  
Note that
\[
  h(w)\leq C(e^w-1)^2\quad (w\geq-1),
  \qquad
  h(w)\leq |w|\quad (w<-1).
\]
This, combined with $e^{W_N} - 1 = Y_N(\beta)-1$ and
\eqref{eq:WN-negative-tail}, yields
\begin{equation*}
  \E h(W_N) 
  \leq    
  C\E (Y_N(\beta)-1)^2
  +\E\bigl[|W_N|\, \mathbf1_{\{W_N<-1\}}\bigr]
  \leq
  C\delta_N^2\sqrt{\log(1/\delta_N)}.
\end{equation*}
Moreover,
since $h(W_N) = Y_N(\beta)-1-W_N$ and $\E(Y_N(\beta)-1) = 0$,
we have $-\E W_N = \E h(W_N)$.
Therefore, by \eqref{eq:EWN range},
we deduce that 
\begin{equation*}
  0 \leq -\E W_N \leq C\delta_N^2\sqrt{\log(1/\delta_N)}.
\end{equation*}
From this and \eqref{eq:deltaN asymp},
it follows that 
\[\E W_N
  =O\!\left(N^{2-q}\sqrt{\log N}\right)
  =o(N^{-(q/2-1)}),
\]
which completes the proof.
\end{proof}
